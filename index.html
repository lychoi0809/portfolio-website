Bilingual Evaluation of Tone and Emotional Nuance in Korean–English Translation: Human vs. AI

Investigated how Korean–English bilinguals evaluate translation quality across human and AI outputs. Conducted Likert-style ratings and open-ended responses with 20 participants evaluating a Korean folktale. Found that participants prioritized emotional authenticity over literal accuracy, with only 60% correctly identifying AI involvement, suggesting translation quality depends on how each approach handles specific cultural challenges, not whether it was human or machine-produced.

field
psycholinguistics · translation studies
languages
korean, english
method
likert ratings · open-ended response
status
completed
read published paper ↗ Oxford Journal of Student Scholarship · July 2026 DOI: 10.65161/recY0BFnDqYI2Chrq
The act of translation requires navigating tensions between fidelity to source texts and accessibility for target audiences. This challenge is especially present in Korean-English translation, where there is substantial linguistic and cultural distance. In an era where AI translation systems grow ever more sophisticated, the question arises regarding how bilingual readers perceive and evaluate translation quality across human and AI-generated outputs. This paper investigates how Korean-English bilingual speakers evaluate tone, emotional nuance, and cultural meaning in translation, and what criteria they prioritize when assessing translation quality. Twenty bilinguals evaluated excerpts from the Korean folktale The Tiger and the Dried Persimmon, comparing a human translation with a ChatGPT-4o generated version through Likert-style ratings and open-ended responses. Findings suggest that participants tended to prioritize emotional authenticity and tonal fidelity over literal accuracy, and considered cultural appropriateness largely context-dependent. While AI translation was preferred for conveying character voice and humor, neither translation adequately rendered culturally-specific expressions such as aigoo or yayya. Notably, participants could not reliably distinguish AI from human translation, with only 60% recognizing AI involvement, suggesting that translation quality depends on how each approach handles specific challenges rather than whether it was produced by human or AI.

Frequency Accelerates Semantic Change: Evidence from 500 Years of Korean

Challenged the "law of conformity," the long-held assumption that high-frequency words resist semantic change. Trained diachronic word embeddings on historical Korean corpora spanning the 15th to 20th centuries and found the opposite: high-frequency words changed more, not less. Validated findings across six robustness controls and an English replication, linking the reversal to grammaticalization and semantic bleaching processes unique to Korean typology.

field
historical linguistics · corpus linguistics
language
korean + english replication
timespan
15th–20th century · 500 years
status
completed
read published paper ↗ ACL Anthology · NLP4DH Workshop, 2026
The "law of conformity," the finding that frequent words are semantically stable, has been treated as a broad regularity of language change. We show it does not hold for Korean. Using diachronic word embeddings trained on historical corpora spanning 500 years (15th–20th centuries), we find a robust positive correlation between frequency and semantic shift: high-frequency Korean words change more, not less. The pattern survives six robustness controls and is validated against an English replication. Partial correlation analysis reveals that the role of polysemy in mediating the frequency–change relationship is not fixed but depends on time resolution and corpus homogeneity. We connect the reversal to frequency-driven reductive processes, including grammaticalization, semantic bleaching, and domain shift, that are especially productive in Korean. The frequency–change relationship is not a fixed regularity but varies with language typology and analytical conditions.

Characteristics and Species Diversity of S. angulatus Colonies in the Imjin River Basin of the Western Civilian Control Line

Conducted field research in the western civilian control line to analyze the spread and ecological impact of bur cucumber (Sicyos angulatus), an invasive species threatening native riparian vegetation across South Korea. Surveyed three habitat types along the Imjin River and found the species concentrated in riverbank and forest-edge environments. Results contribute to conservation understanding of one of the world's most ecologically unique and understudied landscapes.

field
ecology · conservation biology
species
Sicyos angulatus
location
imjin river · western ccl
status
completed
Bur cucumber (Sicyos angulatus) is an invasive plant species in South Korea that disrupts ecosystems by inhibiting the growth of native plants and destroying riparian vegetation. Given that S. angulatus is considered a harmful species, this study analyzed its impact on the species diversity of the ecosystem in the Imjin River basin of the Western Civilian Control Line. We limited the study to certain areas along the Imjin River, where S. angulatus was primarily found: (1) in regions with flowing water along riverbanks and streams, and (2) in the second most common habitat along forest edges, whereas (3) it was almost absent in agricultural fields and flatlands. It was observed that the riverbanks create an ideal environment for various species, including S. angulatus, due to their damp and sunlit conditions.

Embolization of Brain Aneurysms Using Pluronic-Based Polymer Coils

Developed and characterized PluCoil, a novel injectable hydrogel system for brain aneurysm embolization, as part of the Garcia Summer Research Program at Stony Brook University. The study explored mechanical optimization of a two-part pluronic system, achieving 83% coiling efficiency — roughly fourfold higher than conventional platinum coils — while demonstrating biocompatibility and successful embolization in vivo.

field
materials science · biomedical engineering · neuroscience
program
garcia summer research program, stony brook university
status
completed · summer 2026
submitted · Materials Research Society Conference
Intracranial aneurysms are localized cerebral blood vessel dilations that may rupture and cause life-threatening hemorrhage. Endovascular embolization aims to isolate the aneurysm from blood flow while preserving the parent vessel, but conventional coil-based treatments are limited. PluCoil is a novel, biocompatible, injectable, two-part pluronic hydrogel system: a thermoresponsive F127DMA physical gel followed by an F88DMA coil crosslinked via an ammonium persulfate (APS)/tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) redox system. This study explores the characterization and optimization of PluCoil's mechanical properties to improve performance and application.

Leachate analysis via UV-vis showed a burst leaching model plateauing between 2h and 1d, and cytotoxicity assays on fibroblasts yielded healthy cell morphology and density across all conditions. Rheological temperature sweep revealed a reduction in sol-gel transition temperature and increased post-gelation shear viscosity as gel concentration increases. Injectability studies measured pressure change over time across three catheter sizes. Dimensional stability was improved with multiblock and tetramer additives, with tetramer providing stronger long-term mass and volume retention despite greater initial swelling. An average coiling efficiency of 83% was identified — roughly fourfold higher than conventional platinum coils. Successful middle meningeal artery embolization was achieved both in vitro and in vivo at 20% F127DMA and 20 mg/ml APS. Together, these results establish PluCoil as a tunable, biocompatible injectable hydrogel with strong potential for aneurysm treatment and broader injectable regenerative engineering applications.

Igbo Language Textbook

a textbook written from scratch to support igbo heritage classes in korea, for children reconnecting with the language their families carry from home.

[ add photos of your drafts, the community, or textbook pages here ]

In a Nigerian community in Korea, many parents speak Igbo and English fluently while their children, raised here and educated in Korean schools, have grown up with Korean as one of their strongest languages. Some speak little or no Igbo. Community members recently started Igbo classes to address this, but beginner-level teaching materials simply don't exist for this context.

I'm developing those materials. Having spent time with the children and families, I'm building something that meets them where they are: designed around the languages they already know, the culture they're reconnecting with, and the reality of learning a heritage language as a child growing up between worlds.

What Is Lost When Translating Literature from One Language to Another?

Starting from the experience of reading Yoon Dong-ju's poems first in Korean and then in English, and the quiet disappointment of the second encounter, examined how grammar, honorifics, structural logic, and cultural implication create meaning that cannot survive intact across languages. Drew on Korean, Japanese, Chinese, and French literature to argue that translators must always choose between fidelity to words or fidelity to feeling.
